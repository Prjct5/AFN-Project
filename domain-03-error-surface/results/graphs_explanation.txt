Graph Analysis Notes
====================


Graph 1 – Confidence KDE (Correct vs Incorrect Predictions)
------------------------------------------------------------
This plot shows how confident the model is when it gets things right versus when it
gets them wrong. The blue peak (correct predictions) is incredibly sharp and sits
right at 1.0, which means the model is almost always near-certain when it predicts
correctly. The orange curve (incorrect predictions) is flatter and spreads from
roughly 0.4 all the way to 1.0.

The real issue here is that flat orange tail near 1.0 — the model is making wrong
calls while still being highly confident. That's a calibration problem. High
confidence doesn't mean correct prediction, and that's something worth addressing
before deployment.


Graph 2 – Spearman Correlation Heatmap
---------------------------------------
A few things stand out here. Text length and word count are almost the same feature
(correlation = 0.89), so keeping both in a model would be redundant. Confidence
has a mild negative correlation with both (-0.29 and -0.26), meaning the model
gets slightly less confident on longer texts — makes sense intuitively.

The is_error vs confidence relationship (-0.17) is weak but there. Errors tend to
happen at slightly lower confidence levels, though not by much. URL count and
emoji count show almost no meaningful correlation with anything, which suggests
they don't carry much signal for this task.


Graph 3 – Failure Mode Distribution
-------------------------------------
Four error types are shown here, and the distribution is pretty telling.

High Confidence Failures top the chart at around 246 cases. These are the most
dangerous errors — the model is wrong and doesn't know it. Calibration Instability
comes second at ~178 cases, meaning the model gives inconsistent confidence scores
for similar inputs. Trigger Word Dependency sits at ~131, where the model leans on
specific words rather than understanding the full context. Short Context Failures
are rare (~9 cases), so text length isn't really a driver of errors here.

The takeaway: most failures are confidence-related, not comprehension-related.


Graph 4 – MARBERTv2 Confidence Distribution
---------------------------------------------
Almost everything is stacked in the 0.95–1.0 bin, with correct predictions
(green) reaching over 45,000 cases there. Incorrect predictions (red) are barely
visible, not because errors don't exist, but because they're swamped by the volume
of correct ones.

This histogram makes the class imbalance obvious. It also shows why raw accuracy
is a misleading metric here — the model predicts one class so frequently and
confidently that the numbers look great on the surface.


Graph 5 – Confusion Matrix
----------------------------
Numbers:
  - True Positives  (Fake  predicted as Fake): 35,365
  - True Negatives  (Real  predicted as Real): 11,609
  - False Negatives (Fake  predicted as Real):    302
  - False Positives (Real  predicted as Fake):    209

Overall accuracy lands around 99.1%, which looks strong. But the 302 false
negatives are worth paying attention to — those are fake articles the model let
slip through as real. In a misinformation detection context, that's the more
costly mistake compared to false positives.


Graph 6 – ROC Curve (AUC = 0.565)
------------------------------------
This is where things get interesting. A 99% accuracy model should not have an AUC
of 0.565 — that's barely above random (0.5). The ROC curve hugs the diagonal for
most of its range, only pulling away near the top right.

What this tells you is that the confidence score itself is not a reliable signal
for separating correct from incorrect predictions. The model classifies well in
absolute terms, but if you try to use its confidence score as a threshold to
flag uncertain cases, it won't help much. This disconnect between accuracy and AUC
is a direct consequence of the calibration issues seen in graphs 1 and 3.


Graph 7 – Structural Binning: Accuracy Across Text Length Groups
-----------------------------------------------------------------
The dataset was split into five word-count quintiles, ranging from very short texts
(under 11 words) to longer ones (up to 45 words). Accuracy is stable across all
five groups, sitting between roughly 99.3% and 99.8%.

There's a slight downward trend as texts get longer, but it's marginal — nothing
that would suggest the model struggles with a particular length range. Text length
doesn't appear to be a meaningful source of errors here.


Graph 8 – Top Trigger Words in Error Set (TF-IDF)
---------------------------------------------------
These are the Arabic words that appear most frequently in the error cases, ranked
by TF-IDF score. The top words are all prepositions and common function words.
Normally TF-IDF would downweight these, so their high scores here suggest the
error set might not have been filtered for stop words, or that these words
genuinely co-occur with the harder-to-classify samples.

Lower on the list: sharika (company), riyal, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, and
al-tawasul (communication/social media). This points to a cluster of errors around
Saudi financial or business content, which could mean the model hasn't seen enough
of that domain during training, or that content from that region is structurally
harder to classify.


====================
Summary
====================
MARBERTv2 hits strong accuracy numbers (~99%), but the analysis reveals a model
that's overconfident and poorly calibrated. The AUC of 0.565 is the clearest
symptom — the confidence score barely correlates with prediction correctness.
Most errors fall into the high-confidence failure category, which makes them hard
to catch without additional post-processing. The trigger word analysis also hints
at a domain gap around Arabic financial/Saudi content that could be worth
addressing with targeted data augmentation.
